Apostille First or Translation First? The Order Foreign Registries Expect

It feels like diligence to translate first. It is the most common sequencing error in marriage registration abroad — the apostille belongs on the original, and the sworn translation follows it.

The expensive version of the mistake

A couple preparing to register their marriage in Spain has the certificate translated into Spanish first — it feels like diligence. They use a certified translator at home, then have the translation notarized, then ask for an apostille on the package. What arrives at the Spanish registry is an apostille certifying a notary's signature on a translation the registry never asked for, attached to a marriage certificate that still has no apostille of its own. The filing goes back. The certificate still needs its apostille, and the translation still needs to be redone — this time by the translator the registry actually recognizes.

Why the apostille goes first

The logic follows from what an apostille is. It authenticates the signature of the official who issued the original document — the clerk or registrar who certified the marriage record. A translation is a new document, created by a translator, about the original. Apostilling a translation authenticates nothing about the marriage; it authenticates the translator's paperwork. Meanwhile, most destination countries reserve legal translation to their own sworn translators — Spain's traductores jurados, Italy's sworn traduzione asseverata process — and a sworn translator works from the document in its final, apostilled form, translating the certificate and the apostille together. Translation performed before the apostille translates a document that is about to change.

The order that holds

For the large majority of registries, the sequence is: certified copy of the marriage certificate from the issuing authority; apostille on that original; then sworn translation in the destination country, of the certificate with its apostille attached. Each step certifies the step before it, and the registry receives a chain with no gaps.

The honest exceptions

Some authorities publish their own order and it wins. Certain consulates accept certified translations from the applicant's home country; a few destinations ask for translation at a different point in the chain; non-Hague legalization routes have their own sequencing again. The general rule is the default, not the law — the destination registry's or consulate's published requirement for your specific filing is the instruction to follow, and it is worth reading before paying for either step. What never varies: an apostille placed on a translation instead of the certificate is not a shortcut. It is the same money spent on the wrong document.

When a marriage certificate package comes to us, the order of operations is part of the review — certificate, apostille, then the right translator in the right country.
