Las Vegas Marriages: The Clark County Certificate Chain

Foreign couples marry in Las Vegas and fly home with the keepsake. The legal chain runs through a filing deadline nobody mentions and two different county and state offices — here is the order that works.

Three documents, one wedding

A Las Vegas wedding produces three pieces of paper, and foreign couples routinely fly home with the wrong one. Before the ceremony there is the marriage license — permission to marry, issued by the Clark County Marriage License Bureau. During the ceremony the officiant and witnesses sign the marriage certificate — the record that a marriage took place. And at the chapel there is usually a decorative keepsake, suitable for framing, with no legal existence at all. The document a home-country registry will ask for is none of these in raw form: it is a certified copy of the filed certificate, and for use abroad, that copy carrying a Nevada apostille.

The ten days nobody mentions

The certificate does not become a public record when the officiant signs it. Under Nevada law, the officiant has ten calendar days to deliver the signed certificate to the Clark County Clerk for filing — the county's own guidance states the deadline plainly. Until the certificate is filed, no certified copy can be issued, because there is nothing on record to copy. Couples who marry on a Saturday and visit a government office on Monday expecting a certified copy are asking for a document that does not yet exist. Reputable chapels file promptly; the trap is not the wait, it is not knowing there is one.

Clerk for the copy, Secretary of State for the apostille

Once the certificate is on file, certified copies are issued by the Clark County Clerk — the county calls them proof of marriage. The apostille is a separate step at a separate office: the Nevada Secretary of State apostilles the certified copy. The Clerk does not issue apostilles, and the Secretary of State does not issue the underlying copy. Foreign couples who send the chapel keepsake, the license, or an uncertified copy to the Secretary of State get the package back, and couples who ask the Clerk for an apostille are asking the wrong desk. Two offices, two documents, in that order.

What the home registry does with it

The apostilled certified copy is the instrument the home country works from — to register the marriage, to update civil status, to support a spouse's residency or a name change where the home system allows one. Many registries will also require a sworn translation, performed in the destination country after the apostille; the sequence matters for the same reasons it matters everywhere else. What the home registry will not work from is the keepsake, a photograph of the certificate, or the license — the three artifacts couples most often carry home.
